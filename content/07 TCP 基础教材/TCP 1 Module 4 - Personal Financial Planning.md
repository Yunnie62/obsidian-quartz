# TCP 1 Module 4 - Personal Financial Planning

## Module Purpose

This module covers tax-aware personal financial planning: retirement plans, IRAs, annuities, investment risk, education funding, insurance, after-tax return on investment, and estate planning.

## H - Highlight

### Retirement Planning

- 401(k) employee elective deferral for 2023: $22,500; $30,000 if age 50 or older.
- Combined employee and employer 401(k) limit for 2023: $66,000; $73,500 if age 50 or older.
- IRA contribution limit for 2023: $6,500; $7,500 if age 50 or older.
- Traditional retirement contributions generally create tax benefit now.
- Roth contributions generally create tax benefit later.
- Premature taxable distributions may trigger a 10% additional tax before age 59 1/2, subject to exceptions.
- Roth qualified distributions generally require age 59 1/2 and a five-year holding period.

### Education Planning

- Section 529 qualified tuition programs may be prepaid tuition plans or educational savings plans.
- Qualified education distributions can be tax-free.
- Excess distributions may create taxable earnings and a 10% additional penalty.
- Student loan interest deduction is limited to $2,500.
- Scholarships and fellowships can be tax-exempt when requirements are satisfied.

### Insurance and Risk

- Risk management methods include avoidance, reduction, retention, and transfer.
- Long-term care benefit triggers include inability to perform at least two activities of daily living for at least 90 days, or substantial cognitive impairment.

### Investment Return

- ROI compares gain or loss to investment cost.
- After-tax rate of return adjusts the investment return for tax.

### Estate Planning

- Wills do not override beneficiary designations.
- Beneficiary designations can transfer assets directly.
- Joint tenancy with right of survivorship can transfer ownership outside probate.

## A - Ask

- Is the account traditional or Roth?
- Is the contribution deductible, nondeductible, or after-tax?
- Is the distribution taxable, nontaxable, or partially taxable?
- Does a penalty apply?
- Does an exception apply?
- Is the education distribution qualified?
- Is investment risk systematic or nonsystematic?
- Does estate transfer happen through probate, beneficiary designation, or joint ownership?

## R - Rephrase

1. Traditional accounts usually shift tax to later; Roth accounts usually pay tax now for potential tax-free treatment later.
2. Retirement distribution questions require account type, contribution type, age, holding period, and exception analysis.
3. Education accounts are tax-favored only to the extent distributions are used for qualified expenses.
4. Insurance planning is about matching risk severity and frequency with the right risk management method.
5. Estate planning depends heavily on legal ownership and beneficiary designations.

## S - Synthesize

### High-Frequency Rules

- Identify the account type before deciding taxability.
- Roth treatment depends heavily on qualified distribution rules.
- Self-employed retirement plans use different contribution formulas and caps.
- Education account taxation depends on qualified expenses and earnings portion.
- Long-term care insurance uses ADLs and cognitive impairment as benefit triggers.
- Beneficiary designations often control asset transfer regardless of a will.

### Common Traps

- Treating Roth contributions as deductible.
- Forgetting that traditional retirement distributions are generally ordinary income.
- Applying the 10% penalty without checking exceptions.
- Treating all 529 distributions as tax-free.
- Ignoring whether scholarship funds were used for qualified expenses.
- Assuming a will controls assets with beneficiary designations.
